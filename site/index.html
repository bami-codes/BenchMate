The drawing is right. The board is right. The output is wrong.

BenchMate gives Muse Spark a real bench. It reads the schematic, chooses experiments on the real circuit, must forecast each one before an Arduino measures it, and keeps crossing out explanations until one is left. Five faults were planted on five boards and sealed; the model found where each board disagreed with its drawing, and every step is a record you can check. It is built for the moment every lab prototype, repair bench and first production board goes through: the drawing is right, the board is right, the output is wrong.

Five cases from real forum threads

Each case rebuilds a problem whose cause was settled in the thread, scaled to the bench: the first power-on moment, when the drawing is right and the board still misbehaves. Every case has its own circuit on the mini board and its own drawing; all use the same procedure, the same test menu and the same fixture. Each replay is a recorded run played back from its log in order: the dashed forecasts are drawn, then the measured trace sweeps over them and the needles move. The bench

One Arduino, two op-amp followers, a reference, and a mini board that holds the circuit under test. About $40 of parts. The model never touches it: it asks, the operator moves one jumper, the Arduino measures.

Source
Arduino UNO R4 Minima DAC through an LM358 follower and a 1 kΩ drive resistor. Steps of ±0.2 / ±0.5 V and sines of 1–5 Hz, from a fixed menu Spark picks from.
Recorder
14-bit ADC, 512 frames per capture at 0.5 ms, three channels: IN, buffered OUT, REF. Captures are rejected across reboots; each run starts from a fresh boot id.
Reference
A buffered divider near 1.63 V is the return for the circuit under test. A guard checks it on every capture; a drift above 3 mV stops the run and nothing is scored.
Allowance
9 mV RMS between forecast and measurement, on the whole step and on every region of it. Frozen before the first run and never changed.
Operator
Connects or removes the 9.99 kΩ load when Spark asks, USB off; takes one multimeter reading when Spark asks; never tells the model what the fault is.
Cost
About ₩56,000 ($40) of parts including spares, plus a multimeter. Build notes.

What this is a prototype of

The thing being tested here is not a circuit. It is whether a model can do the basic move of experimental science on a real system: say what it expects before the measurement, be held to it, and keep going until one explanation is left. A circuit was the cheapest real system where that could be done honestly in one week.

Why a circuit first

A circuit comes with a drawing, a simulator anyone can run (ngspice), an instrument that costs $40, and faults that can be planted and sealed. Every part of the loop could be checked by a stranger with the repository and a multimeter. Nothing else we could build in a week had all four.

What the loop needs, and nothing more

  • a description of what was intended (here: a schematic image),
  • a space of explanations the model may propose (here: a changed value, or a part the drawing lacks),
  • a simulator that turns an explanation into a forecast (here: ngspice),
  • an instrument with a fixed menu the model chooses from (here: steps and sines, with or without a load),
  • a person for what the instrument cannot do (here: move one jumper, read one part),
  • a sealed truth to score against (here: the operator's assembly record, hashed before the first measurement).

None of these six is specific to electronics. The 9 mV allowance, the menu and the calibration are specific to this bench, and would have to be qualified again anywhere else.

What it would take elsewhere

Not done here; stated so the scale of the step is clear.

A real PCB at first power-on
The same loop with the schematic and layout as the description, SPICE as the simulator, and a bench supply, scope and source-measure unit as the instrument menu. The reading stage already exists: it is the probe in the operator's hand.
A thermal or mechanical rig
The description is a CAD or thermal model, the simulator a finite-element solver, the instrument thermocouples and actuators. Forecast first, held-back check, sealed fault: unchanged.
A lab experiment
Explanations are candidate models of the phenomenon; the menu is the settings the apparatus allows; the forecast is pushed before the run. The model chooses the next experiment the way it chose the loaded step in case 01.

The experiments on this page did not exist until the model asked for them. That is the capability being shown; the circuit is the smallest honest place to show it.

How a run works

The same order every time. Only the drawing changes.

1Read the drawing

Muse Spark vision turns the schematic image into parts and connections. A person confirms the reading. The model gets this drawing and the shared bench description, nothing else.

2Choose an experiment

Muse Spark picks from a fixed menu of steps and sines, with or without a 9.99 kΩ load, and says why. The runner never picks for it.

3Forecast first

Every explanation still standing predicts the next measurement. The forecast is hashed before the board is touched.

4Measure and cross out

The Arduino UNO R4 fixture measures the real circuit. Explanations that miss by more than 9 mV, on the whole step or any region of it, are ruled out. If none survive, Spark may propose a part the drawing does not show.

5Check on held-back inputs

The final forecast is pushed to GitHub, then two reserved inputs that were never used for fitting are measured.

6One reading at the bench

When two explanations give the same waveforms, Spark names one part to read. Its expected range under each explanation is frozen first; the operator types one value; the same rule rules out.

7Open the seal

The hidden fault, committed as a digest before the first measurement, is compared with the result by code. SAM 3.1 outlines the bodies in the board photo; Spark vision points at the part.

Who does what

Muse Spark 1.3 vision
Reads the schematic into parts, values and connections, with a box per part. After the diagnosis, looks at the board photo with SAM 3.1's bodies and says which body is the part to read (cases 01, 03, 05), or that it cannot tell (case 02).
Muse Spark 1.3 agent
Chooses every experiment from the fixed menu and says why; asks for the bench to be rewired; decides when to ask for the check; proposes a part the drawing lacks when every value explanation has failed (cases 04, 05); chooses which part to read and which quantity (cases 01, 02, 03, 05).
SAM 3.1
Segments the mini board photo into bodies with one generic prompt, after the diagnosis. Its masks are shown as drawn; no part value is read from the photo, ever.
Fit engine, ngspice
Fits each explanation to the measurements and forecasts the next one. Compares forecasts with the bench by RMS miss. Freezes and publishes before every scored measurement.
Bench, Arduino UNO R4
Two LM358 buffers, a 1 kΩ drive resistor, a 1.63 V reference and the ADC channels. A reference guard stops a run when the bench itself drifts.
Operator
Builds the circuit with the sealed fault, moves one jumper when asked, reads one value when asked, and never tells the model what is wrong.

Built during the hackathon

Everything on this page was made between 27 October and 4 November 2026 by one person and two coding agents: the instrument firmware, the bench, the fit engine, the Spark tool loop, the reading and seal protocol, the photo stage, this site, and the five physical runs themselves. The first commit is the start of the project.

Meta models

Muse Spark 1.3 (agent and vision) through the Meta Model API for every experiment choice, structure proposal, schematic read and body call; SAM 3.1 for every board segmentation. 101 Spark calls and 6 SAM calls across the five cases, about $1.01.

What is new

The model is not asked to explain a measurement someone made. It designs the measurement, commits to its forecast first, and is scored by code against a fault it was never told. The experiments did not exist until the model asked for them.

What is not claimed

Five completed cases are not an accuracy rate. Failed and abstained attempts are kept in the public ledger and named on the reports. Restored reruns show "no mismatch in the tests Spark chose", not a proof that every part is correct. The cases were known to the developers; this is not a blind test.
